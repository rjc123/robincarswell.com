My mother kept the flour and the sugar in Miracle Whip jars. Gallon jars, proper thick glass, heavy metal lids, the sort of thing you could drop and worry about the floor. They came from the early seventies and they lasted until the lids finally rusted through and you couldn't buy replacements. She threw them out when we moved in the nineties. 

Here's the odd part. We never bought Miracle Whip. Not once that I can recall. But for twenty-odd years two gallon jars of it sat in the pantry, brand facing out, quietly advertising a product nobody in the house ate.

### That's what reusable packaging actually does

It outlives the purchase. The jar stops being packaging and becomes furniture, and the logo on the side keeps working long after the sandwich spread is gone. Decades of free advertising, in a home that was never even a customer. Try buying that kind of shelf life with a media plan.

### The Classico trick

Early 2000s, I noticed Classico, the spaghetti sauce, Kraft-owned. Squarish bottles, brassy lids, sturdy and washable and genuinely useful for storing things. And when the lid finally gave out, you could screw a standard Mason jar lid straight onto the thread. Mason-compatible. Somebody at Kraft decided that on purpose.

So now you have a branded jar, "Classico" minted into the glass, sitting in the cupboard with raisins in it, looking back at you, reminding you to have spaghetti for dinner. And because you'd quite like a matching set, you buy more sauce. The reuse doesn't cost them a sale. It *is* the sale. No loyalty card, no gimmick, just a cupboard that wants to match itself.

### So what has this got to do with the UK?

Everything over here comes in tiny packets at eye-watering prices. You cannot buy in bulk. A decent quantity of almost anything, and you're either priced out or it simply isn't on the shelf. And all of it is single-use and disposably flimsy: the half-slit bag of sugar that tips over and goes everywhere the moment you touch it. I keep thinking back to the pantry in that farm house, where you bought a big amount of something, tipped it into a jar, and never thought about it again.

### The opportunity

Two ways to build it.

One: a UK store brand standardises its packaging. One jar system across the whole range, jam and pickles and rice all in the same glass, lids interchangeable, everything stacking in tidy rows. No replacement-lid business to run, because the replacement lid is a fresh jar of jam. Make the thread Mason-compatible while you're at it and you've bought a decade of goodwill for the price of a tooling change.

Two: a challenger brand does it properly. Better glass, built to be reused for a lifetime, logo emblazoned in the side so it stares out of ten thousand pantries for the next thirty years. Bank on the fact that a big store brand will eventually copy the format. Theirs will be plain. Yours will have your name in the glass, permanently.

And once the jars are already in the cupboard, the refill stops needing to be a jar at all. Post me a big decantable packet of the stuff, product depending, and I tip it into the glass I already own. The durable bit lives in my kitchen for a decade; the thing that travels is just contents in the cheapest sensible wrapper.

And then the bit I like best. Good jars become collectible. They end up behind hipster cocktail bars and holding some startup's small-batch pickles. They circulate, they get a second and third life, they carry the logo into rooms your marketing budget will never reach.

### Reduce, reuse, recycle, in that order

The order isn't alphabetical and it isn't an accident. Reduce first. Reuse second. Recycle last, because recycling is the weakest of the three, the thing you fall back on once you've failed at the other two. We've built an entire retail system that sprints past the first two Rs, lands on the weakest one, and calls it virtue.

The jar solves for the middle R, the one everybody forgets. Someone should build it, preferably with their name in the glass giving them credit for the idea.

Full disclosure: I haven't done any mathematics on what this does to a cost line. I'm fairly confident the bean counters will kill it on sight, because they *just don't like marketing psychology*.
