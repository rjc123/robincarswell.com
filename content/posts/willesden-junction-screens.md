There's a spot at Willesden Junction where you come up the stairs from the low-level platforms, and the departure screen for the whole station is right there above the gate line. Every time I pass it I do the same thing: glance up mid-stride, fail to get anything useful out of it, and either guess or slow down and actually stop to read it properly. For a station I use regularly, I still can't tell you my platform from a glance. That's not a me problem.

### What the screen is actually showing you

The screen at Willesden Junction, like most UK station entry screens, is one list, ordered by time, with every platform mixed in together. Something like: 16:32 Platform 2 to Clapham Junction, 16:34 Platform 4 to Watford Junction, 16:35 Platform 1 to Stratford, 16:37 Platform 3 to Euston, and so on, scrolling.

That's a perfectly good way to answer "what's the next train from this station, in general?" It's a bad way to answer the only question almost everyone at that screen actually has, which is "which platform do I need, and how long have I got?" To answer that from a mixed, time-ordered list, you have to read every line, hold the platform numbers in your head, and mentally re-sort the whole thing by platform yourself — while walking, with a bag, possibly with a kid, past a screen you have maybe three seconds in front of.

### The three-second test

Here's the test I think the redesign has to pass, because it's the only real-world condition the screen is used under: **what does someone walking past, not stopping, pick up in about three seconds?**

Three seconds is roughly a fast page-flick of attention: enough to register a word, a number, maybe two. It is not enough to read six scrolling lines and cross-reference them against your own platform. If the answer to "which platform, which direction, how long" doesn't fit in that window, the screen has failed the one job it actually has at that spot in the station: not "publish the timetable" but "let someone moving past make a decision without stopping."

A composite, time-ordered list can't pass that test no matter how it's formatted, because the information a specific passenger needs (their platform) is buried among information they don't (everyone else's platform). Fixing that isn't a font-size problem or a colour problem. The screen needs to organise around a different unit entirely.

### The redesign: organise by platform and direction, not by time

The screen should be laid out as fixed panels, one per platform, each headed by the platform number and the direction it serves, not the time. Under that, the next couple of departures for that platform only.

![Mockup comparing the current time-ordered scrolling screen against a proposed layout split into one panel per platform, each headed by its direction](/images/willesden-junction-redesign-mockup.svg)

The difference isn't cosmetic. In the current version, "Platform 2" is a fact buried inside a scrolling sentence that also tells you about three other platforms. In the proposed version, "Platform 2 → Clapham Junction" is the heading of a panel that never moves and is never interleaved with anything else. Someone who already knows their platform (most regular passengers) reads one word, the direction, confirms it's still on time, and keeps walking. Someone who doesn't know it yet scans four headings instead of parsing six scrolling lines. Either way, the fact that answers their actual question is the first thing on the panel, not something extracted from a list built around a different question entirely.

This is also why it has to be platform-and-direction, not platform alone. "Platform 2" means nothing to someone who's never used the station. "Platform 2 → Clapham Junction" is decodable on sight, because direction is the thing passengers actually hold in their head: nobody thinks "I need platform 2", they think "I need the Clapham Junction train."

### The bit that generalises

None of this is specific to one station. It's a mismatch between what an information display is organised around and the actual moment someone reads it. A time-ordered feed is the right structure for a monitoring wall someone watches continuously. It's the wrong structure for a screen that gets three seconds of attention from someone who already knows which of four fixed categories (their platform) they care about. The screen already has every piece of information anyone needs; it's just anchored to the wrong category. Re-anchor it to the one the reader actually walks in holding, and stop making them re-derive it from a list built for someone else's question.

I'll keep glancing up at that board and getting nothing out of it until somebody redraws it around the platform, not the clock.
