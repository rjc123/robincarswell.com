For years I paid someone a few hours a month to catch invoicing mismatches by hand: a line that didn't reconcile, a rate that had drifted, a client billed for the wrong month. It never once occurred to me to build a tool for it. The problem was real but small, and small problems don't clear the bar for "is this worth building." Six months into this sabbatical I built the fix in an afternoon, almost as an aside, and it's been quietly right ever since. The task hadn't got any less real. What changed was the price of fixing it.

### The bar was never about the problem

Every backlog has a graveyard in it: the ideas that got a nod and then quietly died. Not because they were bad. Because someone ran the sum: a developer's day rate against ten minutes saved a week. The sum said no. That "no" wasn't wrong. It was correct, given what building used to cost. The mistake is assuming it's still correct now that the other side of the sum has moved.

### It isn't speed. It's a different backlog

I used to think getting faster meant clearing the same list quicker. It doesn't work like that. Collapse the build time from a fortnight to an afternoon and the list itself changes shape: a whole tier of ideas that never cleared the old bar clears the new one without anyone having to argue for it. My CRM had 4,223 duplicate company records rotting in it. Nobody had ever proposed cleaning it properly, because "hire someone to manually review four thousand records" was never going to win a budget conversation. One afternoon and a script later, it was done. Nobody had to make the case for it at all.

### The graveyard reopens whether you look at it or not

This is the part I didn't expect. An old "no" doesn't sit quietly waiting to be reconsidered. It rots. The moment the cost assumption behind it stops being true, that "no" is wrong, and it stays wrong, unnoticed, until somebody goes back and rechecks it. Every list of things a business decided weren't worth the effort was scored against a build cost that's since fallen through the floor. Nobody goes back and rescopes a rejected idea from three years ago. It just sits there, correctly filed as "not worth it" under economics that no longer apply.

### This isn't just my backlog

I don't think this is a personal quirk of having six months and a capable model. Any business sitting on a pile of small internal fixes is sitting on the same stale arithmetic: the report someone compiles by hand every month, the reconciliation nobody automated, the one process with an annoying manual step in the middle. The fixes were always technically possible. They just never cleared the cost bar at the old price. At the new price, a meaningful slice of that pile clears it easily, and most of it has been sitting there so long nobody thinks to check any more.

### The catch is deciding, not building

None of this means build everything that's now cheap enough. Some of what's in the graveyard was correctly killed on the merits, not the cost, and cheap building doesn't fix a bad idea. But that's a different, harder problem than the one I'm describing here. It's the one about who actually decides, and it deserves its own essay rather than a bolt-on paragraph at the end of this one. What I'm pointing at is narrower: a huge amount of *genuinely good, genuinely wanted* work has been sitting unbuilt for years for no reason except that the sum used to say no. That sum has changed. Almost nobody has gone back to redo it.

So go back through whatever list your business keeps of the fixes that were never worth the time, and rerun the numbers at this year's build cost, not last year's. Some of it will still be correctly dead. I'd bet more of it than you think isn't dead at all. It was just priced out, and nobody's checked the price since.
